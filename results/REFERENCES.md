# References and claim checks

Checked 28 Sep 2026 against primary sources (arXiv PDFs, ACL Anthology, PMLR,
NeurIPS proceedings, Hugging Face model/dataset files, tiktoken source).
Quotes are copied from the PDF text. Page numbers are the printed page numbers
in the PDF.

## TLDR

- **BrahmicTokenizer-131K is real, and the 26.7% number is real, but the notes
  describe it wrongly.** The 26.7% is measured against a *different* tokenizer
  (Mistral-Nemo Tekken / Sarvam-m) at the same 131K size. It is not the gain
  from the retrofit itself. The tokenizer also shrinks from 200,019 to 131,072
  entries, so it is not fixed-size relative to its source. No model was
  trained, and embedding rows were not re-initialised.
- **Stage 1 of BrahmicTokenizer deletes all 1,561 Thai tokens from o200k_base.**
- **The claim "untested on a non-delimited script" is false.** Csaki et al.
  (2023) already did a fixed-size replace-the-least-frequent-tokens retrofit
  **for Thai** (about 5,000 tokens swapped in a 50K GPT-2 vocabulary, Thai
  fertility improved 73%). Typhoon's "5,000 tokens" and "diminishing returns
  past 5,000" both come from that paper. This must be cited, and the novelty
  claim must be narrowed.
- **Typhoon trained a 5,000-token Thai SentencePiece model, but the released
  vocabulary grows by only 3,219 (32,000 → 35,219).** The paper does not
  explain the gap. Its "2.62x" is measured against GPT-4's tokenizer, not
  against Mistral.
- **Your pre-tokenizer finding is confirmed from source code.** cl100k_base and
  Qwen2.5 use `\p{L}+`, which does not include Thai combining marks (`\p{M}`).
  o200k_base *does* include `\p{M}`, so o200k's 12.8% mark-split rate comes
  from the vocabulary, not the regex.

## 1. Claim checks

### Claim 1: BrahmicTokenizer-131K, 26.7% fewer tokens, fixed size, LP allocation

**Verdict: corrected.** Name and number are right. The baseline, the
"fixed-size" framing and the "embeddings" question all need correcting.

Source: Shravan, R. (2026). arXiv:2605.29379v1, submitted 28 May 2026. It is a
single-author technical report from The School of AI, India. It is not
peer-reviewed. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29379

**The number and its baseline** (Abstract, p. 1):
> "On 27 million documents of public Indic pretraining text (2.84 billion
> words, 46.21 GB), BrahmicTokenizer-131K produces 26.7% fewer tokens than
> Mistral-Nemo Tekken / Sarvam-m at the same vocabulary budget, with
> per-language savings of 15.79% (Tamil) to 76.79% (Odia, a 4.31×
> compression ratio)."

Section 5.1, p. 10 gives the exact figure as 26.75% (6,623M vs 9,041M tokens).
The same section says this test is in-distribution:
> "The 27M-corpus result is therefore an in-distribution measurement of
> BrahmicTokenizer-131K's compression behavior." (Section 5.1, p. 9)

What this means for us: **26.7% compares two different tokenizers. It is not
a before-vs-after of the retrofit.** The paper gives no headline number for
BrahmicTokenizer vs its own source (o200k_base). From its own tables I derived
two rough numbers. These are my calculations, not the paper's:
- Bytes per token on Brahmic text: 1.83 vs o200k_base 1.74 (Table 6, p. 12),
  so about **5% fewer tokens** than o200k_base.
- FLORES-200 mean Brahmic fertility: 2.84 vs GPT-OSS-120B 3.10 (Table 4,
  p. 11). The paper says GPT-OSS-120B is "structurally identical to
  o200k_base" (Section 2.4). That gives about **8% fewer tokens**.

**English preserved** (Abstract, p. 1): confirmed.
> "On non-Indic content, BrahmicTokenizer-131K matches o200k_base's English
> fertility (1.235 versus 1.232 tokens/word)"

Section 4.1, p. 7: 63,919 vs 63,941 tokens on FLORES English, "a 0.034%
reduction in total token count". Note that English is measured in tokens per
**word**.

**Is it fixed-size? Only partly.** It has two stages (Section 3.2, p. 5):
> "Stage 1 reduces o200k_base's 200,019-token vocabulary to 131,072 by
> removing 38,345 tokens covering nine non-target scripts: CJK Unified
> Ideographs, Hangul (Korean), Hiragana+Katakana (Japanese), Arabic,
> Cyrillic, Thai, Greek, Hebrew, and Sinhala."

Table 1 (p. 5) lists **Thai: 1,561 tokens removed**. Only Stage 2 is
fixed-size: 2,372 of 131,072 slots are replaced (1.81%, Section 7.2, p. 17).
The embedding matrix changes shape (Abstract, p. 1):
> "The embedding matrix resizes from 200,019 to 131,072 rows -- a standard
> vocabulary-resize operation that downstream training already handles."

**Prune rule** (Section 3.4, p. 6; Section 6.2, p. 16): slots were chosen by
how often they fired on a 1.045-billion-token Indic audit corpus. 2,083 tokens
fired zero times. The other 289 fired 1–1,000 times and were
> "tokens for out-of-scope content classes that we chose to remove by policy."

**How new tokens were chosen** (Section 3.6, p. 7):
> "New entries were generated by training BPE on the audit corpus restricted
> to the Brahmic-script subset and selecting the top-N merges that satisfied
> the no-cross-script-merge rule (no merge combining bytes from two disjoint
> writing systems)."

2,156 new merges were **appended at the end** of the merge list. Slot mix
(Section 3.5, p. 6; Table 8, p. 13): 5 "character infrastructure" slots, 1,443
word-level slots, and per-script content plus 149 numerals and 3 punctuation
tokens.

**Linear program: yes, but it is solved greedily and did no better than a
heuristic** (Section 3.5.1, p. 6):
> "The LP objective maximized total token-savings on the audit corpus subject
> to per-script feasibility constraints."

The objective is: maximise Σ_s c_s(x_s), subject to Σ x_s = 2,215 and
0 ≤ x_s ≤ K_s. Here c_s is the cumulative-savings curve for script s, and K_s
is the size of that script's candidate pool. Because the curves are concave,
> "the problem therefore reduces to a discrete concave-objective allocation
> and admits a greedy solver"

Table 12 (p. 15) shows the shipped allocation was actually a hand-tuned
heuristic ("Shipped (heuristic)"). The LP gave the same total savings
(+0.00%). Section 6.1:
> "The specific per-script ratios are not load-bearing; the shape of biasing
> toward under-resourced scripts is."

**Embeddings and retraining: not done at all** (Section 2.6, p. 5):
> "we do not perform model-side training to consume the new vocabulary -- the
> artifact ships as a drop-in for o200k_base such that a downstream training
> pipeline absorbs the new entries through its normal embedding-learning
> dynamics. The construction is therefore a pure tokenizer-side intervention"

So there is **no model evaluation and no embedding initialisation method**.
Our project's "swapped rows need re-initialising" caveat goes further than
BrahmicTokenizer does.

**Relevant limitation** (Section 7.3, p. 17): 569 of the 2,156 new merges
(26.4%) never fired on the audit corpus.

**Why a retrofit works here:** BrahmicTokenizer keeps o200k_base's
pre-tokenizer, and the o200k regex includes `\p{M}` (see Claim 3h). A Brahmic
vowel sign therefore stays in the same pre-token as its consonant. This is
exactly the condition that cl100k and Qwen2.5 fail for Thai.

### Claim 2: Typhoon added 5,000 Thai tokens; 2.62x; diminishing returns

**Verdict: partly confirmed, partly corrected.**

Source: Pipatanakul et al. (2023), arXiv:2312.13951v1, 21 Dec 2023.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13951

**5,000 tokens** (Section 3.2 "Thai Tokenizer", p. 4):
> "In this work, we base our tokenizer on Mistral-7B tokenizer, but we further
> train an additional Thai subword tokenizer with 5,000 tokens and integrate
> it with the original tokenizer. This new tokenizer is created by training a
> SentencePiece model [17] on around 8 million samples randomly selected from
> the Thai subset of MC4 data [60]."

**Final vocabulary size:** the paper's own Table 1 (p. 3) gives Typhoon-7B a
VocabSize of **35,219**. The HF `config.json` for `scb10x/typhoon-7b` also
says `"vocab_size": 35219`.

**Reconciling 5,000 with 3,219.** I checked the HF tokenizers (scratch script,
28 Sep 2026):
- All 32,000 Mistral entries keep the same IDs in Typhoon.
- 3,219 new entries sit at IDs 32000–35218. 3,131 of them contain Thai
  characters.
- Mistral already had only 57 entries containing Thai.

So the project notes should say: **"trained a 5,000-piece Thai SentencePiece
model and merged it in, giving a net 3,219 new entries."** The paper never
explains why 1,781 pieces were dropped. Overlap with Mistral's existing Thai
entries (57) cannot explain it. It may be de-duplication against existing
non-Thai or character pieces, or filtering, but **this is uncertain and not
stated in the source.**

**2.62x** (Abstract, p. 1; Section 3.2, p. 4):
> "Our experimental results in Table 2 show that Typhoon's tokenizer is 2.62
> times more efficient than GPT-4."

The baseline is **GPT-4's tokenizer (cl100k_base), not Mistral**. Table 2
(p. 4) lists Typhoon 262%, GPT-4 100%, Mistral 93%, Character 89%, Word
(newmm) 326%, OpenThaiGPT 350% and WangChanGLM 305%.

Two cautions:
- Equation 1 is printed as #token_model / #token_GPT-4. The table values only
  make sense as the inverse (GPT-4 tokens ÷ model tokens).
- The paper does not say which Thai text was used for the measurement.

Derived, not in the paper: against Mistral, 2.62 / 0.93 ≈ **2.8x fewer tokens**.
That is roughly in line with our own baseline (Mistral 3.92x penalty vs
Typhoon 1.26x, a ratio of about 3.1).

**Diminishing returns** (Section 3.2, p. 4):
> "Previous work demonstrated that adding beyond 5,000 new Thai tokens yields
> a diminishing return in efficiency [11]."

[11] is Csaki et al. (2023). Typhoon did not measure this itself.

**How Typhoon's new embeddings were set up:**
- The paper only describes the usual practice, citing others: "the tokenizer
  of the original model is usually augmented with new tokens where the
  corresponding embeddings are initialized from scratch [36, 11]" (Section
  3.2).
- It says: "we continue pretraining the extended embeddings and the language
  modelling head of Mistral-7B using LoRA" (Section 3.3, p. 5).
- Its initial experiments trained "the embeddings and the language modeling
  head only" (Section 3.3, p. 4).
- It does **not** say exactly how Typhoon's own new rows were initialised.

HF model card (https://huggingface.co/scb10x/typhoon-7b) repeats "2.62 times
more efficient in tokenizing Thai text". It gives no token counts.

### Claim 3: Other prior work

#### NEW and critical: Csaki et al. (2023), fixed-size token replacement for Thai

**This is direct prior art for our method on Thai.** Source: arXiv:2311.05741v2.
The comments say it was accepted to the ENLSP-III workshop at NeurIPS 2023.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05741

Section 2.1, p. 2:
> "In our work, instead of extending the tokenizer's vocabulary, we replace
> the least frequent tokens from it with tokens from the new language. This
> way, we keep the model capability the same by controlling the vocabulary and
> embedding table size. In particular, we train a BPE tokenizer on the new
> language with vocabulary size k and check the number of overlapping tokens o
> with the original tokenizer. Then we replace the least important k − o
> non-overlapping tokens from the original tokenizer with the new ones. We also
> reinitialize the corresponding embeddings in the model."

Also Section 2.1, p. 2:
> "We choose to replace around 5000 tokens, which is only 10% of the overall
> vocabulary, because it improves the fertility by 42% on Hungarian and 73% on
> Thai. [...] In addition, replacing more tokens beyond 5000 provides
> diminishing returns on the fertility, while also increases the difficulty of
> model adaptation due to having more randomly re-initialized token
> embeddings."

Other details:
- Appendix A.2, step 5: "Randomize the embeddings of the replaced tokens".
- The new merges are added **to the beginning** of merges.txt. BrahmicTokenizer
  appends them at the end.
- The base model is an in-house 13B GPT-2-architecture model with vocabulary
  50,260 (Appendix B).
- Thai fertility is per word on the Thai CoNLL 2017 UD treebank (Appendix A.1).
- The paper continues pretraining with a 50/50 Thai/English mix.

What this means for us:
- "Untested on a non-delimited script" must go.
- Our differences from Csaki are:
  - an existing public tokenizer (cl100k, BLOOM and others) rather than GPT-2;
  - a per-character metric;
  - three separate domains;
  - separating pre-tokenization effects from vocabulary effects;
  - no GPU.

#### (a) Embedding initialisation for new tokens

- **FVT (Gee et al., 2022):** if a new token is already in the old
  vocabulary, it keeps its old embedding. Otherwise the new token is split
  with the old tokenizer, and its embedding is the **average** of those
  pieces' embeddings. Section 3:
  > "If instead t_i ∈ V_in \ V_gen, then the in-domain embedding is the
  > average of the embeddings associated with the tokens produced by T_gen"

  The paper used this for domain-specific compression of BERT-style models,
  followed by MLM training. This is the "mean of sub-token embeddings" method.
- **WECHSEL (Minixhofer et al., 2022):** replaces the tokenizer with a
  target-language one. Each new token's embedding is built from source-token
  embeddings that are close in an aligned multilingual static word-embedding
  space. It reports "up to 64x less training effort" than training from
  scratch (abstract).
- **FOCUS (Dobler & de Melo, 2023):** from the abstract, it "represents newly
  added tokens as combinations of tokens in the overlap of the source and
  target vocabularies. The overlapping tokens are selected based on semantic
  similarity in an auxiliary static token embedding space." It uses sparsemax
  weights and XLM-R as the source model.

#### (b) Petrov et al. (2023), tokenizer unfairness (NeurIPS 2023)

- **Thai: not found in the paper text.** I searched the full arXiv v2 PDF and
  "Thai" does not appear. Per-language values are in the authors' online
  materials, which I did not check.
- Main claim (abstract): "differences up to 15 times in some cases". For the
  ChatGPT/GPT-4 tokenizer (cl100k), Shan is worst at about 15x (Section 4.1).
- Their Shan example has the same mechanism as our mark-split finding
  (Section 4.1, p. 5):
  > "This word is constructed from one consonant and three diacritics. As the
  > diacritics are encoded separately, there are four Unicode codepoints for
  > this Shan character, resulting in 9 tokens."
- They define "premium" as |t(s_A)| / |t(s_B)| on FLORES-200 parallel
  sentences (Section 3). This is the same idea as our "penalty".

#### (h) Pre-tokenization effects

**Regex check from source, confirmed:**
- cl100k_base, from tiktoken `tiktoken_ext/openai_public.py`:
  `'(?i:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)|[^\r\n\p{L}\p{N}]?+\p{L}++|\p{N}{1,3}+| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]++[\r\n]*+|\s++$|\s*[\r\n]|\s+(?!\S)|\s`
- Qwen2.5-7B, from `tokenizer.json` pre_tokenizer (Split):
  `(?i:'s|'t|'re|'ve|'m|'ll|'d)|[^\r\n\p{L}\p{N}]?\p{L}+|\p{N}| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]+[\r\n]*|\s*[\r\n]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+`
- o200k_base: its letter classes are `[\p{Lu}\p{Lt}\p{Lm}\p{Lo}\p{M}]` and
  `[\p{Ll}\p{Lm}\p{Lo}\p{M}]`, so **marks are included**.

I tested "ภาษาไทยที่สวย" with the Python `regex` module:
- cl100k splits it into 3 pre-tokens `[Lo×8] [Mn+Mn] [Lo×3]`. The marks ี + ่
  end up alone.
- o200k keeps it as 1 pre-token.

**So the claim is confirmed for cl100k and Qwen2.5.** For o200k, the 12.8%
mark-split rate cannot come from the regex, so it is a vocabulary effect. This
means the mark-split rate is only a pure pre-tokenization measure for
tokenizers whose regex leaves out `\p{M}`. Check BLOOM, Mistral and XGLM
before treating it as a pre-tokenization measure for them.

**Papers:**
- **Dagan, Synnaeve & Rozière (2024), ICML, PMLR 235:9784–9805.** The abstract
  says "the size, pre-tokenization regular expression, and training data of a
  tokenizer can significantly impact the model's generation speed, effective
  context size, memory usage, and downstream performance". It also says:
  "when fine-tuning on more than 50 billion tokens, we can specialize the
  tokenizer of a pre-trained LLM to obtain large gains". The work is about
  code, not Thai. Velayuthan & Sarveswaran say they took the GPT-4 regex from
  this paper.
- **Velayuthan & Sarveswaran (2025), COLING 2025, pp. 5987–5996.** The arXiv
  version is from 2024 (arXiv:2409.11501). The abstract says pre-tokenization
  "has a more significant impact than tokenization algorithms". Section 3.1:
  > "the pre-tokenizer effectively bounds the maximum token length that can be
  > formed for a given pre-token."

  Their Table 1 shows GPT-2, GPT-4 and Llama 3 pre-tokenizers breaking Tamil,
  Sinhala and Hindi words at vowel signs. They do **not** name `\p{M}`; that
  diagnosis is ours. They propose Grapheme Pair Encoding (GPE).
- **Schmidt, Reddy, Tanner & Pinter (2025), COLM 2025, arXiv:2504.00178.**
  "Boundless Byte Pair Encoding: Breaking the Pre-tokenization Barrier". It
  lets BPE merge across pre-token boundaries ("superwords"). I checked only
  the abstract. It is optional background.
- **Regmi, Pudasaini & Pun (2026), arXiv:2608.26449** (added 5 Oct 2026; the
  mid-term proposal cited it, the first check missed it). "Vowel Signs Are
  Not Letters: A Pre-tokenization Ceiling on Multilingual Tokenizer
  Fertility". Checked from the arXiv v1 PDF:
  - Abstract: the GPT-2 word class `\p{L}+` "splits each word at every vowel
    sign" in abugidas; across 26 languages "ranging from 1.47x (Tibetan) to
    9.02x (Thai)"; o200k's mark-aware class makes "the repair itself prior
    art".
  - Table 4 caption: "Shatter ratio is pre-tokens under \p{L}+ over
    pre-tokens under [\p{L}\p{M}]+", on FLORES-200 devtest.
  - Table 6 (appendix A), Thai row: 9.02x; 37.4 vs 4.1 **pre-tokens** per 100
    characters. These are a floor on the token count (their Proposition 1),
    not token counts.
  - They build a Nepali-English tokenizer and train 268M models; they do not
    decompose existing tokenizers' vocabularies or study Thai beyond the
    bound.
  - **Consequence:** the `\p{L}+` / `\p{M}` diagnosis is theirs first. This
    project's decomposition confirms it for Thai on real tokenizers and adds
    the vocabulary share and the Qwen2.5 unreachable entries.
- **MEGA (Ahuja et al., 2023, EMNLP)** and **"Measuring the Tokenization
  Premium" (Roy, Roy & Patel, 2026, arXiv:2608.09046)**, both cited in the
  proposal: checked from their arXiv abstracts. MEGA benchmarks generative
  models across 70 languages and does not isolate tokenizer cost; the
  premium paper covers Bengali, Hindi, Arabic, Tamil and Yoruba, not Thai.
  Neither is used in the report.

## 3. Method notes

**How BrahmicTokenizer-131K built its vocabulary.** It starts from OpenAI's
o200k_base (200,019 entries). First, it deletes every token belonging to
nine scripts it does not need (CJK, Korean, Japanese kana, Arabic, Cyrillic,
**Thai**, Greek, Hebrew, Sinhala). That removes 38,345 tokens, leaving 131,072.
Next, it runs this cut-down tokenizer over a 1-billion-token Indic corpus and
finds 2,372 slots that almost never fire. It trains BPE on the Indic part of
that corpus. It keeps the top merges that never join two scripts, and splits
the free slots between the nine Brahmic scripts. The split is chosen by a
greedy "give the next slot to the script that saves the most tokens"
procedure, which the paper calls an LP; a hand-tuned rule gave the same
result. New merges are appended after the old ones. The pre-tokenizer regex
and decoder are unchanged. No model was trained, and the paper proposes no
way to initialise the new embedding rows. It is a tokenizer-only artifact.
Its headline 26.7% is against Mistral-Nemo's Tekken tokenizer at the same
131K size. Against its own source, o200k_base, the gain on Indic text is
about 5–8% by my reading of its tables.

**How Typhoon built its vocabulary.** Typhoon started from Mistral-7B's
32,000-entry SentencePiece tokenizer. It trained a separate 5,000-piece Thai
SentencePiece model on about 8 million Thai samples from mC4 and merged it
in. In the released tokenizer this gives **3,219 new entries** (IDs
32000–35218), for a total of 35,219. The paper does not explain the gap from
5,000. This is **expansion**: every original Mistral token keeps its ID, and
the embedding matrix and LM head grow. The model was then further pretrained
on a 50/50 Thai/English mix. The expanded embeddings and LM head were trained
with LoRA. The "5,000" size is justified by citing Csaki et al. (2023), who
found diminishing returns past about 5,000 new Thai tokens. Csaki et al.
reached that result with a **fixed-size replacement** scheme, which is the
same family as this project's method.
