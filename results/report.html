Retrofitting Thai into an open-weight tokenizer at fixed vocabulary size

Tanabodhi Mukura (Dean) · CEDT, Chulalongkorn University · AI course project · draft generated 28 September 2026 from the numbers in results/

TL;DR

  • Thai costs Mistral-7B 3.92x as many tokens as English for the same meaning, and all of that damage is in its vocabulary: its pre-tokenizer never cuts Thai text, so a vocabulary retrofit can reach all of it.
  • Swapping 2,483 of its 32,000 entries (7.8% - every entry that raw English, Python code and Thai never use) for Thai pieces cuts Thai tokens by 64% on Wikipedia, 67% on news and 61% on social posts, takes the penalty to 1.33x, and changes English by +0.01%. The vocabulary stays the same size. At Typhoon's own budget (3,219) the fixed-size retrofit beats Typhoon's vocabulary expansion on all three domains.
  • GPT-4's cl100k and Qwen2.5 cannot be fixed this way. Their regex matches letters with \p{L}+, which excludes Thai vowel and tone marks, so every mark is cut off its consonant before the vocabulary is consulted. Qwen2.5 even holds 1,578 Thai words it can never emit; all are untrained, so the one-line regex fix alone makes Qwen2.5-0.5B 2-3x worse at Thai.
  • The price is real and stated: informal Thai gains less than formal (spread 1.11, the same as Typhoon; 1.21 if the vocabulary is learned from formal text only), and the freed slots come from other languages - Korean text costs 39% more tokens, Russian 18%, Chinese 13%, Japanese 10%.
  • The swapped embedding rows must be re-set, but that is cheap. On BLOOM-560m, leaving them as they were nearly doubles Thai bits per character and random re-initialisation quadruples it; setting each row to the mean of the pieces it replaces recovers most of the loss with no training, and 200 steps training only those rows ends better than the original on Thai (-9 to -14% bits per character, with the caveat in §7.6) at a third of the tokens, with English unchanged. On the target model, Mistral-7B, a CPU-sized pass is not enough - it already reads Thai well - but 400 QLoRA steps on a laptop GPU bring the retrofitted Mistral-7B level with an equally trained control on news and within ~9% on Wikipedia and social, at a third of the Thai tokens (§8.5). "No retraining" is narrowed to: no expansion and no pretraining from scratch, but the swapped rows must be re-set and trained.
  • Several earlier claims did not survive checking and are corrected here: the Week 1 mark-split metric, the cl100k recommendation, "every tokenizer costs more on social text", and the novelty of fixed-size replacement for Thai (Csaki et al. did it in 2023).

1. Problem

Thai is written without spaces between words and is thinly represented in the data most tokenizers were trained on, so the same meaning costs far more tokens in Thai than in English. Tokens are what a model is billed, limited and timed by: a tokenizer that needs four times as many tokens for Thai makes Thai four times as expensive per request, fits a quarter as much Thai into the context window, and generates it four times more slowly.

On 3,000 matched Thai/English sentence pairs (OPUS-100), the Thai penalty - Thai tokens per 100 characters divided by English tokens per 100 characters - ranges from 4.25x (BLOOM) and 3.92x (Mistral-7B) down to 1.01x (mT5) (BASELINE.md). The penalty is worst in open-weight models, which are the only ones whose tokenizer a deployment can change at all: a hosted API's tokenizer is fixed by the provider.

The prior Thai answer, Typhoon (SCB 10X), expanded Mistral-7B's vocabulary with Thai entries and continued pretraining. That grows the embedding matrix and the output layer, which is reasonable if you are training a model anyway. This project asks the narrower question a self-hosting team faces: can an existing open-weight tokenizer be made cheap for Thai without growing it - by pruning entries the deployment never uses and putting Thai units in the freed slots - and what does that actually cost?

2. Research questions

  1. How much of each tokenizer's Thai penalty is caused by its pre-tokenizer (a regex problem no vocabulary can fix) and how much by its vocabulary (the problem a retrofit targets)?
  2. For a tokenizer whose damage is in the vocabulary, how much Thai cost does a fixed-size retrofit remove, per domain, while leaving English untouched?
  3. How does that compare with vocabulary expansion (Typhoon) on the same base?
  4. Who pays? Does the retrofit widen the gap between formal and informal Thai, and which other languages lose the freed slots?
  5. What do the swapped embedding rows need before a model can use them?

3. Prior work, and what is new here

Every claim below was checked against the primary source; quotes, page numbers and the corrections are in results/REFERENCES.md.

Tokenizer unfairness. Petrov et al. (2023) show the same content can cost up to 15 times more tokens in some languages, and their Shan example - one consonant plus three diacritics becoming nine tokens - is the same mechanism this project finds for Thai. Rust et al. (2021) define the per-word fertility measure used here as a secondary figure.

Vocabulary expansion for Thai. Typhoon (Pipatanakul et al., 2023) trained a 5,000-piece Thai SentencePiece model and merged it into Mistral-7B's tokenizer. The released tokenizer grows by 3,219 entries (32,000 → 35,219; every Mistral id unchanged) - the paper does not explain the gap from 5,000. Its reported 2.62x efficiency gain is measured against GPT-4's tokenizer, not against Mistral. The expanded embeddings and output layer were then trained with LoRA during continued pretraining.

Fixed-size replacement - including for Thai - already exists. Csaki et al. (2023) replaced about 5,000 of the least frequent tokens of a 50k GPT-2 vocabulary with Thai BPE tokens, randomly re-initialised the swapped rows, and report 73% better Thai fertility with diminishing returns past ~5,000 tokens (Typhoon's "5,000" cites them). The project notes' claim that the method is "untested on a non-delimited script" is therefore wrong and has been dropped. BrahmicTokenizer-131K (Shravan, 2026) does the same for Indic scripts; its 26.7% headline is measured against a different tokenizer (Mistral-Nemo's Tekken) at equal size, not before/after its own retrofit, and its first stage shrinks o200k from 200,019 to 131,072 entries - deleting all of o200k's Thai tokens along the way.

Embedding initialisation. FVT (Gee et al., 2022) initialises a new token as the mean of the embeddings of the old tokens it used to be split into; WECHSEL (Minixhofer et al., 2022) and FOCUS (Dobler & de Melo, 2023) use aligned or auxiliary embedding spaces.

Pre-tokenization. Dagan et al. (2024) and Velayuthan & Sarveswaran (2025) show the pre-tokenizer regex bounds what a vocabulary can do; the latter show GPT-4-style regexes breaking Tamil, Sinhala and Hindi words at vowel signs.

What this project adds, relative to that work:

  1. A per-tokenizer decomposition of the Thai penalty into pre-tokenization and vocabulary shares across nine tokenizers, with the specific cause named: \p{L}+ in the cl100k and Qwen2.5 regexes excludes Thai combining marks (\p{M}).
  2. The finding that Qwen2.5 already contains 1,578 Thai vocabulary entries its own pre-tokenizer can never emit, that all of them are untrained, and what it takes to make them usable.
  3. A fixed-size retrofit of a current public open-weight tokenizer (Mistral-7B) with English and code non-regression by construction (a protect corpus rather than "least frequent"), a merge-safe prune rule, a published prune list, and a like-for-like comparison with Typhoon's expansion of the same base.
  4. Per-register results (encyclopaedic, news, informal social), a formal-only ablation, and the collateral cost to the languages whose slots are freed.
  5. A CPU-scale test of what the swapped rows need: stale vs random vs mean-of-parts initialisation, and row-only tuning.

4. Data

All data is public and openly licensed, streamed from Hugging Face and cached locally. Evaluation always uses the first 1,200 usable documents of each Thai domain (the Week 1 slice); anything that learns from text uses documents after them, so nothing is tuned on the text it is scored on.

5. Metrics

Primary: tokens per 100 characters. Thai has no whitespace word boundaries, so tokens-per-word depends entirely on which word segmenter you pick; per-character cost does not. Segmenter-based fertility is reported as a secondary figure under two segmenters (newmm, longest) to show how much the segmenter moves it.

Thai penalty: Thai tok/100ch ÷ English tok/100ch on matched pairs (the same measure as Petrov et al.'s "premium").

Effective context: Thai characters per 1,000 tokens.

Cluster-break rate (replaces Week 1's mark-split rate): the share of Thai character clusters (a base character plus its following vowel and tone marks) that the final tokens split, measured on byte offsets. Week 1's measure decoded one token at a time, which silently skipped every token that was a fragment of a 3-byte Thai character.

Bits per character (BPC): a model's total negative log-likelihood in bits divided by characters. Unlike perplexity it does not depend on how the text was split, so it compares one model under two tokenizers fairly.

6. Method

6.1 Decomposing the penalty (Week 2)

Source: src/pretok.py, src/run_decomposition.py.

A BPE tokenizer works in two stages: a pre-tokenizer cuts the text into pieces (with a regex, or on whitespace), then the vocabulary encodes each piece on its own. No token can span a pre-token boundary. So for each of nine tokenizers the pre-tokenizer was read from its own configuration and run directly, and three counterfactual token counts were computed per domain:

  • T_regex: the same vocabulary and merges behind a mark-aware regex (every \p{L} rewritten to [\p{L}\p{M}], the change o200k made). This is the regex fix on its own.
  • T_vocab: a strong Thai vocabulary (XGLM's) applied inside the tokenizer's own pre-token pieces. This is the best any vocabulary retrofit could do while the pre-tokenizer stays as it is.
  • T_ref: that same strong vocabulary with no pre-tokenizer constraint.

The gap between the shipped count and T_ref is the excess over a good Thai tokenizer. Of that gap, T_vocab − T_ref is lost to the pre-tokenizer (no vocabulary recovers it) and the rest is recoverable by vocabulary. The plan's original floor - one token per cluster - was dropped because good Thai tokenizers already use fewer tokens than there are clusters, which made the plan's share formula negative.

6.2 Candidates (Week 3)

Source: src/candidates.py.

Thai training text was segmented into newmm words and Thai Character Clusters (TCC). Frequencies were computed per million characters in each domain and averaged, so each domain carries equal weight regardless of size. Each unit was scored by savings(u) = freq(u) × (base_tokens(u) − 1), and the projected reduction was measured on the held-out evaluation slice per domain.

6.3 The retrofit (Week 4)

Source: src/retrofit.py.

Freeing slots. A vocabulary entry may be freed only if (a) it is never produced when encoding the protect corpora - raw English Wikipedia (line breaks kept) and public Python code - nor the Thai training text, (b) it is not a special, byte-fallback or base-byte token, and (c) every merge that uses it as a part produces an entry that is also being freed, so no kept entry loses the path that builds it. Among eligible entries the highest id goes first (BPE and SentencePiece assign ids in learning order, so late ids are the rarest units). The prune list is published per budget (results/prune_list_*.csv).

Filling slots. Thai characters the base cannot represent at all (26 for Mistral, including the repetition mark ๆ and the tone marks ๊ ๋, which Mistral spells as three byte tokens) are added first as single-character entries. Then BPE training is continued on the Thai training text from the base tokenizer's own segmentation: at each step the adjacent pair whose merge saves the most tokens (domain-weighted) becomes a new entry, and its merge is appended after all existing merges. This is the Week 3 greedy-by-savings rule applied one slot at a time. It pays for intermediate pieces out of the same budget, and it satisfies the plan's requirement that every new token be producible, because each one is built from pieces that already exist. Because new merges come last, every existing merge keeps its priority and text with no Thai in it is segmented exactly as before.

Rebuild. The new vocabulary (pruned entries removed, new entries in the freed ids, lowest id first) and merge list are assembled with the tokenizers library's BPE model and saved as a standard Hugging Face tokenizer folder. Vocabulary size is identical by construction.

A mistake found and fixed. The first prune list protected only English prose (with whitespace collapsed by the corpus loader), so it freed code and LaTeX pieces such as func, json, Override and mathcal that an English-speaking deployment still needs. The protect corpora are now raw English text plus raw Python; this cut Mistral's free slots from 4,471 to 2,483. The same protect set is also offered without code (the "English-only" profile, up to 4,339 slots) to show what the code protection costs Thai.

6.4 Evaluation (Week 5)

Source: src/evaluate.py, src/verify_retrofit.py.

Every tokenizer is scored on held-out text only: the Thai evaluation slice per domain, the OPUS-100 pairs, and 4,000 raw English Wikipedia documents disjoint from the protect corpus. Changes carry 95% percentile-bootstrap intervals over documents (1,000 resamples). Acceptance follows the plan: Thai tokens down at least 15% in every domain, English within 1%, effective context up at least 15%.

6.5 Embedding adaptation (Week 6)

Source: src/embed_adapt.py, src/qwen_repair.py.

On BLOOM-560m (CPU), retrofitted with 5,000 Thai entries, the swapped rows were set in five ways: left stale; re-drawn at random (as Csaki et al. did); set to the mean of the base-token embeddings the new token used to be split into (FVT, Gee et al. 2022); and each of the last two followed by training only the swapped rows on Thai text with every other parameter frozen (200 Adam steps of 4 × 256 tokens, learning rate 1e-4, gradients clipped). Each condition is scored by bits per character on held-out Thai (60 passages per domain) and English. The same repair was applied to Qwen2.5-0.5B's untrained Thai rows (§7.5).

7. Results

7.1 The baseline reproduces; one of its diagnostics did not

Re-running the Week 1 harness reproduced all 36 result rows exactly (BASELINE.md). Two of Week 1's interpretations did not survive:

  • The "mark-split rate" was not a pre-tokenization measure. It decoded one token id at a time, so byte-level fragments of Thai characters decoded to U+FFFD and were never counted, and it could not separate a pre-tokenizer cut from a missing merge. It led Week 1 to recommend cl100k as a retrofit target - the opposite of what §7.2 finds.
  • "Every tokenizer costs more on social than on news" is false for the three most expensive tokenizers (Mistral 96.9 vs 99.9 tok/100ch, BLOOM 93.9 vs 98.9, GPT-4 89.2 vs 93.7). It holds for every tokenizer that has Thai vocabulary to lose, and for the strong-vocabulary reference (32.0 vs 28.6), so the informal-Thai premium appears once a tokenizer can compress Thai at all; a character-level tokenizer pays the same everywhere.

7.2 Where the penalty comes from

Source: DECOMPOSITION.md.

Tokenizer Penalty Pre-tokenizer cuts consonant|mark Regex fix alone Best vocabulary alone Pre-tok share of excess
BLOOM 4.25x 0% 4.25x 1.24x 0%
GPT-4 (cl100k) 3.94x 100% 3.94x 2.25x 38%
Mistral-7B 3.92x 0% 3.92x 1.13x 0%
Qwen2.5 2.38x 100% 1.42x 2.25x 89%
GPT-4o (o200k) 1.92x 0% 1.92x 1.27x 1%
Typhoon-7B 1.26x 0% 1.26x 1.13x 0%
XGLM / mT5 / WangchanBERTa 1.16x / 1.01x / 0.42x 0% - - n/a (already near reference)

Penalty on 3,000 OPUS-100 pairs; "regex fix" and "best vocabulary" recompute the Thai side only. Per-domain tables are in DECOMPOSITION.md and agree.

  • cl100k and Qwen2.5 cut every consonant away from its vowel or tone mark in the pre-tokenizer, because their regex matches letters with \p{L}+ and Thai marks are \p{M}. Every other tokenizer cuts none (o200k added \p{M} to its regex).
  • cl100k needs both fixes. Its vocabulary was learned behind the broken regex, so it has no entries spanning a consonant and its mark: fixing the regex alone changes nothing (92.4 → 92.3 tok/100ch on Wikipedia), and a perfect vocabulary alone stops at 2.25x.
  • Qwen2.5 is the opposite: 89% of its excess is the regex, and the regex fix alone takes its token count from 2.38x to 1.42x - because its vocabulary already holds Thai words it cannot reach (§7.5).
  • Mistral-7B and BLOOM carry all of their damage in the vocabulary, where a retrofit can reach it: a strong Thai vocabulary inside their own pre-token pieces would bring them to 1.13x and 1.24x.

Target chosen: Mistral-7B. Its damage is all vocabulary, it is the most expensive fixable open-weight tokenizer after BLOOM, and Typhoon is Mistral-7B expanded - so a fixed-size retrofit of the same base gives a like-for-like comparison. BLOOM (250k entries) is the secondary target because its 560M checkpoint runs on a CPU for the embedding experiment.

7.3 What the candidates promise

Source: CANDIDATES.md.

If each of the top-N newmm words became a single token, Mistral's held-out Thai tokens would fall by about 37% at N = 1,000 and 58% at N = 5,000, with the marginal gain dropping from 17% per thousand (500-1,000) to 2.4% per thousand (4,000-5,000) - the diminishing returns Csaki et al. and Typhoon report. Thai Character Clusters saturate at about 40%: there are only ~5,000 distinct clusters, so they are the right atom but too small a target. The ranking is register-sensitive: ไม่ (not) and แล้ว (already) are five times more frequent in social text than in Wikipedia, and the repetition mark ๆ - which Mistral cannot encode as one token at all - four times.

This projection turned out to be pessimistic, not the upper bound it was first taken for. It assumes each slot buys one whole word; BPE instead builds pieces that are shared by thousands of words (ที่, การ) and can span word boundaries (เมื่อวันที่), so the real retrofit beat it at every budget - at 500 slots, 47% fewer tokens on Wikipedia against a projected 28%.

7.4 The retrofit

Source: RETROFIT_CHECKS.md, EVALUATION.md.

How many slots can Mistral free? Of its 32,000 entries, raw English Wikipedia uses 26,741, Python code 23,471 and the Thai training text 13,582; 29,196 are used by at least one. After the merge-safety rule, 2,483 entries (7.8%) can be freed without touching anything English, code or Thai uses. Dropping the code protection raises that to 4,339 - the "English-only" profile, reported separately because it has a cost (§8.4). Even the strict profile frees fewer slots than Typhoon added (3,219): a 32k vocabulary is tight when English and code are protected.

Acceptance (Week 4). Every retrofit - 500 to 2,483 slots, both ablations, the English-only profile and four BLOOM budgets (1,000-10,000) - keeps the vocabulary size identical, round-trips 10,000 held-out Thai and 10,000 held-out English samples with zero failures, and uses at least 99.3% of its added tokens on held-out Thai. At 2,483 slots, 98 of the top-100 Week 3 candidates encode to a single token; the two that do not (เนื่องจาก, การเลือกตั้ง) cost 2 tokens instead of 9 and 12. This is a near-miss on the plan's literal criterion, caused by the method: BPE continuation adds one merge at a time, and the last merge of a long word saves only one token per occurrence, so it ranks below other merges. At 3,219 slots (English-only) and on BLOOM from 3,219 up, all 100 are single tokens.

English cost does not move. Tokens per 100 characters change by +0.01% on 4,000 held-out raw English Wikipedia documents and +0.11% on the OPUS-100 English side at 2,483 slots (Typhoon: +0.06% and +0.06%). 99.55% of 10,000 English samples get byte-identical token ids, so for them the model's input is exactly what it was before.

Effective context almost triples. Thai characters per 1,000 tokens go from about 1,000 to 2,901 (Wikipedia), 2,997 (news) and 2,655 (social) at 2,483 slots.

Fixed size versus expansion. At the same budget as Typhoon (3,219 slots, English-only profile) the fixed-size retrofit is cheaper than Typhoon on all three domains (−66.3 / −68.6 / −63.0% vs −64.9 / −67.4 / −61.6%) and ties it on the matched-content penalty (1.26x), without growing the embedding matrix. Under the strict profile, with 736 fewer slots, it is within 0.5-0.8 points of Typhoon. Paired on the same documents (vs_typhoon_bootstrap.json, 2,000 resamples), the 3,219-slot retrofit needs 3.7-4.2% fewer tokens than Typhoon in every domain and the strict 2,483-slot retrofit 1.3-2.5% more; every one of these intervals excludes zero. The comparison is of tokenizers only: Typhoon also continued pretraining its model, which this project does not.

Week 5 acceptance: all pass. Every budget clears all three targets in every domain (Thai ≥15% fewer, English within 1%, context ≥ +15%), by a wide margin.

Ablations at 2,483 slots.

  • Cluster-aware (never create a token that begins with a vowel or tone mark): 0 such tokens instead of 211, at a cost of about 1 point of reduction (−63.2 / −65.3 / −59.5%). Worth considering if downstream tools care that token boundaries respect clusters.
  • Formal-only (merges learned from Wikipedia and news only): about 0.5 points better on those two domains, 3 points worse on social (−58.1% vs −61.1%). Its vocabulary spends slots on royal titles, politics and Wikipedia boilerplate (เกล้าเจ้าอยู่หัว, ผู้แทนราษฎร, แหล่งข้อมูลอื่น) where the equal-weight vocabulary has everyday words (ไม่ได้, วันนี้, ตอนนี้, แบบนี้, ลูกค้า).

BLOOM, the secondary target, has far more room: of its 250,680 entries only 77,741 are used by English, code or Thai, and 170,347 can be freed. From BASELINE_after.md (the Week 1 harness re-run with every retrofit added):

Cluster breaks. The share of Thai clusters split by the final tokens falls from 22% to 3.1% (Wikipedia) for Mistral at 2,483 slots - the same as Typhoon (3.1%) - and from 39% to 3.1% for BLOOM at 10,000.

7.5 Qwen2.5's Thai vocabulary it cannot use

Source: QWEN_UNREACHABLE.md.

Qwen2.5's vocabulary contains 2,570 Thai entries. 1,578 of them (61%) can never be produced by its own pre-tokenizer, because each contains a consonant followed by a mark and the regex always cuts there - whole words such as ประวัติศาสตร์ (history) and อย่างต่อเนื่อง (continuously). Over 12.4M characters of Thai the shipped tokenizer emits them zero times.

In Qwen2.5-0.5B (input and output embeddings tied) all 1,578 rows are untrained: every one sits on the same vector as the 271 padding rows no token ever uses (norm 0.301, within 0.1% of the padding centroid), while the 992 reachable Thai rows look like any other trained row (median norm 0.447). A whole-vocabulary check finds the same for all 1,673 entries the pre-tokenizer can never produce - Thai, Arabic with vowel marks, full-width digits - with no exceptions.

So the one-line regex fix from §7.2 is not free. Behind a mark-aware regex, Qwen2.5-0.5B's Thai token count drops by 31-42%, but its bits per character get 2-3 times worse on every domain (Wikipedia 1.42 → 4.30, news 1.78 → 4.69, social 2.69 → 5.53; worse on 50 of 50 passages in each): a third of the new tokens are rows the model has never seen, each costing about 19 bits to predict.

Can the rows be repaired cheaply? Only partly. Each of the 1,578 rows was set to the mean of the embeddings of the pieces the shipped tokenizer splits that word into, and then only those rows were trained on Thai text (200 steps of 8 × 256 tokens; every other tensor verified bit-identical afterwards). Two learning rates were run (QWEN_UNREACHABLE.md, QWEN_REPAIR_lr1e-4.md):

News 34% 1.776 4.687 3.196 2.528 2.286 (+29%)
Social 31% 2.694 5.525 3.868 3.368 3.069 (+14%)
English, Wikipedia - 0.888 - 0.888 0.956 0.888

Mean initialisation wins back about half of the loss for free and leaves English untouched. Row-only tuning at the gentler learning rate wins back 82-87% and still leaves English exactly unchanged; at the twenty-times larger rate it recovered less (about 75%) and pushed English up 8-10%, because the repaired rows - shared between reading and predicting - started taking probability at English positions. So the English leak was the learning rate, not the method. Even so, Thai stays 14-37% worse than with the shipped tokenizer: for Qwen2.5, the regex fix plus row repair is not enough. The rest of the network learned Thai in about 1.6 times more pieces and is frozen here; adapting more than the rows (for example LoRA on the attention layers) is the obvious next test and was not run.

7.6 What the swapped rows need

Source: EMBED_ADAPT_bloom-th5000.md.

BLOOM-560m was retrofitted with 5,000 Thai entries (2.0% of its 250,680 rows) and each way of setting the swapped rows was scored in bits per character on 60 held-out passages per domain (lower is better):

Swapped rows set by Wikipedia News Social English Thai tok/100ch
original tokenizer (reference) 3.312 3.995 3.841 1.132 97.1 / 98.5 / 93.1
left as they were 6.128 6.966 6.814 1.132 28.9 / 32.2 / 31.9
random re-initialisation 13.930 15.921 15.884 1.197 same
random + 200 row-only steps 4.178 4.854 4.697 1.143 same
mean of old pieces, no training 3.934 4.381 4.155 1.132 same
mean + 200 row-only steps 3.028 3.450 3.343 1.132 same

Only the 5,000 swapped rows changed during tuning (verified: 5,000 rows changed, 0 outside the swap). Tuning used 204,800 tokens of Thai training text (200 Adam steps of 4 × 256, learning rate 1e-4).

  • Leaving the rows as they were is harmful: Thai bits per character nearly double, because each new Thai token is read and predicted with the vector of an unrelated pruned token.
  • Random re-initialisation, as Csaki et al. did, is far worse (about four times the original bits per character) and also hurts English: BLOOM ties its input and output embeddings, so 5,000 random rows compete in every English prediction. Even after 200 steps of row-only tuning it has not caught up with the untrained mean initialisation.

  • The mean of the old pieces' embeddings recovers almost everything with no training at all, and leaves English exactly unchanged, while the model reads roughly a third as many Thai tokens.

  • Mean initialisation plus light row-only tuning ends up better than the original on Thai - 8.6% lower bits per character on Wikipedia, 13.6% on news, 13.0% on social - while the model reads about a third as many Thai tokens, and English is unchanged to four decimal places. One caveat keeps this from being a clean win for the tokenizer alone: the reference model got no Thai training in this experiment, while the tuned rows saw 204,800 tokens of Thai from the same three domains (different documents). The defensible claim is that after 200 steps of training only the swapped rows, the retrofitted model is at least as good at Thai as the original, at about a third of the Thai token cost. BLOOM was not pretrained on Thai, so its reference is weak.

  • Contrast with Qwen2.5 (§7.5). There the same recipe, at the same learning rate (1e-4), recovers 82-87% of the loss but does not reach the shipped tokenizer, with English again unchanged. The difference is the reference: Qwen already models Thai well through its shipped tokenizer, while BLOOM barely models Thai at all, so BLOOM has far less to match.

At the target scale: Mistral-7B (MISTRAL_ROWS_mistral-7b-th2483.md). Mistral-7B was evaluated in bfloat16 on the CPU, and the swapped rows were tuned briefly (160 steps of 4 × 128 tokens, one English window per batch, float32 master copies of the rows). With the 2,483-slot retrofit, on 40 held-out passages of ~400 characters per domain:

Swapped rows set by Wikipedia News Social English
original tokenizer (reference) 1.505 2.262 2.267 0.709
left as they were 4.431 5.134 4.973 0.709
mean of old pieces, no training 3.490 4.035 3.903 0.709
mean + 160 row-only steps 3.100 3.543 3.477 0.709

Mistral-7B already models Thai well character by character (1.5 bits per character on Wikipedia, against BLOOM-560m's 3.3), so it has much more to lose: mean initialisation recovers only 32-40% of the damage here, against ~80% on BLOOM. English is untouched in every condition. At the target scale the swapped rows need training, not just a good starting point.

A short row-only tuning pass (81,920 tokens, about an hour on the CPU) recovers 45-55% of the damage and was still improving when it stopped (quick-check Wikipedia 3.54 → 3.18 over 160 steps), with English unchanged. It is nowhere near the original. At 7B scale the swapped rows need a real training budget: with LoRA plus the rows on a laptop GPU (1.6M tokens) the gap to an equally trained control falls to 1-10% (§8.5).

So the honest version of the "no retraining" claim is: no vocabulary expansion and no pretraining from scratch, but the swapped rows must be re-set (the mean of the old pieces is the right start) and trained. For a model that reads Thai poorly (BLOOM-560m) a few hundred row-only steps on a CPU are enough; for one that already reads Thai well (Mistral-7B) a CPU-sized pass recovers about half, and 1.6M tokens of QLoRA on a laptop GPU get within 1-10% of an equally trained control (§8.5) - a budget far below Typhoon's continued pretraining, though the two were not compared on equal terms.

8. Robustness, fairness and cost

All numbers in this section are from ROBUSTNESS.md.

8.1 The word segmenter does not drive any conclusion

Tokens per word was recomputed under PyThaiNLP's longest segmenter next to newmm. longest finds 2-5% more words per 100 characters, so every fertility figure drops by a similar few percent, but the ordering of the nine published tokenizers is identical under both segmenters in all three domains (Kendall's τ = 1.00), and the retrofit's relative gain is the same. Per-character cost remains the headline because it needs no segmenter at all.

8.2 Register: the retrofit widens the formal/informal gap, like every Thai-capable tokenizer

Spread = social tok/100ch ÷ mean of Wikipedia and news.

Mistral-7B (base) 98.4 96.9 0.985

+ 4,339 slots (English-only) 30.0 33.9 1.128
Typhoon-7B 33.3 37.2 1.117

Base Mistral is character-level for Thai, so it charges every register the same (social is even 1.5% cheaper). Every vocabulary that compresses Thai compresses formal Thai more - presumably because formal text is more standardised, though that was not tested. The retrofit's spread (1.110) is in line with Typhoon's (1.117). What the data does show is that the choice of training text matters: a vocabulary learned from formal text alone raises the informal premium from +11% to +21%. Equal domain weighting is not a formality - it is what keeps the informal gap at Typhoon's level instead of twice it.

Protecting code also protects CJK: the strict profile frees 473 CJK entries against 936 without code protection, which is why Chinese moves +12.9% instead of +44.7% (the code corpus evidently contains CJK text, most likely in comments and strings - not checked).

8.5 Showcase: the retrofit in a working model

Source: qwen_adapt_*.json, qwen_showcase.json; live page: the Thai Token Meter.

To show the method end to end on hardware a student has, both interventions were applied to Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, a small open model that already handles Thai: the mark-aware regex (so its 1,578 dead Thai entries become reachable) plus a fixed-size retrofit of 3,000 Thai entries into slots that English, code and Thai never use. On held-out text this tokenizer needs 46-51% fewer Thai tokens than the shipped one (penalty 2.38x → 1.22x), with English unchanged; it passes every Week 4 check (including all 100 top candidates as single tokens and 99.99% of English samples byte-identical).

Fair comparison. Two copies of the model were trained identically on the CPU - same data (Thai text from all three domains, English text, and Thai instructions from WangchanThaiInstruct in the model's chat format), same 400 steps of 8 × 256 tokens, same LoRA (rank 16 on every attention and MLP projection) - one with the shipped tokenizer (control), one with the retrofitted tokenizer, where the 4,578 unseen rows (3,000 added, 1,578 previously unreachable) start at the mean of their pieces and train alongside the LoRA. Any difference between the two arms is the tokenizer's.

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct Wikipedia News Social Thai chat English Thai tok/100ch
original (no training) 1.470 1.858 2.072 1.095 0.993 55 / 60 / 59
control: 400 steps, shipped tokenizer 1.441 1.788 1.951 1.022 0.990 same
retrofit, rows mean-initialised, untrained 2.981 3.453 3.449 2.445 0.993 about half
retrofit: 400 steps, retrofitted tokenizer 1.647 2.042 2.112 1.171 0.988 about half

(Bits per character on held-out text, lower is better; 50 passages per domain, 25 + 25 English passages, 40 held-out chat conversations.)

Training closes most of the gap - from about +100% to +8-14% against the control - but after 400 CPU steps the retrofitted model is still 8-14% behind the control on Thai and 14% on Thai chat, while reading Thai in about half as many tokens and leaving English unchanged. The retrofit arm had 4,578 rows to learn from scratch and the control none, so equal training is not equal starting line; its loss was still falling at the end. In generated answers to held-out instructions the retrofitted model produces about 1.8 times as much Thai text per token (3.7 characters per token against 2.0; 17-29 characters per second on this CPU against 4-16), but a 0.5B model's answers are rough in every arm and the retrofit's are sometimes off-topic: the showcase demonstrates cheaper Thai, not better answers.

The target model with a real training budget (optional GPU extra). The CPU could give Mistral-7B's swapped rows only 160 steps (§7.6). With the author's approval, the same two-arm design was run on a laptop GPU (RTX 4070, 8 GB) in 4-bit NF4 (QLoRA): 400 steps of 8 × 512 tokens (1.6M tokens), LoRA rank 16 on every projection, 6 Thai + 2 English windows per batch; the retrofit arm also trains the 2,483 swapped rows of the input embedding and the output head from their mean initialisation (mistral_adapt_*.json). Every condition is measured under the same 4-bit weights, on the §7.6 passages:

retrofit: 400 steps, retrofitted tokenizer 1.596 2.028 2.223 0.751 ~35%

With 1.6M tokens of training the retrofitted Mistral-7B matches the equally trained control on news (+0.7%) and is ~9% behind on Wikipedia and social, while reading Thai in about a third of the tokens; it is already better than the untrained original on news and social. English rises 2.5% against the control (both arms drift slightly on English because the tuning text is three-quarters Thai). Compared with the CPU run's 160 steps (Wikipedia 3.10), the gap closes from ~100% to ~9%, which answers the question §7.6 left open: at 7B scale the swapped rows can be trained back, and a single laptop GPU is enough to get most of the way. The loss was still falling at 400 steps.

A longer retrofit run (unpaired). The retrofit arm was also trained for 1,000 steps with more English in each batch (6 Thai + 3 English windows; mistral_adapt_retrofit-mistral-7b-th2483_s1000.json). A bug caught while running this (fixed): transformers.AutoTokenizer, given the saved Qwen model folder, rebuilds a Qwen2Tokenizer with Qwen's default \p{L}+ pattern and ignores the mark-aware pattern stored in tokenizer.json (40 ids instead of 23 on a Thai probe). Training loaded the tokenizer from tokenizers/ and was unaffected, but the first retrofit Belebele run and the showcase answers had loaded it from the model folder, so their prompts were split the old way (that run's Thai passages cost 51.7 tokens per 100 characters instead of 26.3). Both were redone with src/hf_tok.py, which loads the saved pipeline and checks it against the tokenizers library; every Mistral folder was checked and is unaffected.

The Thai Token Meter page runs all seven tokenizers in the browser with a 250-line JavaScript BPE that reproduces the originals exactly (identical ids on all 2,114 held-out test texts for every tokenizer), so anyone can paste Thai and see the difference.

9. Limitations and ethics

What "no retraining" can honestly mean. A fixed-size retrofit keeps the embedding matrix's shape, so nothing is added and no full pretraining is needed. It does not mean zero adaptation: each swapped row still holds the vector of the token it used to be, and §7.6 shows that using the retrofit without touching those rows is harmful. The defensible claim is: no vocabulary expansion and no pretraining from scratch; the swapped rows need re-initialising and training - lightly for a model weak in Thai, with a real budget (not measured here) for one that is already strong, such as Mistral-7B.

Who can use it. Only self-hosted open-weight deployments (on-device and edge, or organisations that must self-host for PDPA or data-residency reasons). A hosted API's tokenizer cannot be changed by its users, so GPT-4, GPT-4o or Claude users gain nothing from this method.

A retrofit is deployment-specific. "Unused" means unused by the protect corpora chosen here (English prose and Python). Entries freed for Thai were mostly Cyrillic, other-language Latin-script and markup pieces, CJK and Hangul, so a deployment that also serves those languages would pay for the Thai gain (§8.4). The prune list is published so a deployment can check it against its own traffic.

Scale of the model experiments. Everything except one clearly marked extra ran on a CPU. The full initialisation-and-tuning comparison used small models (BLOOM-560m, Qwen2.5-0.5B); Mistral-7B was evaluated on 40 passages per domain, tuned for 160 steps on the CPU, and - the extra - trained for 400 QLoRA steps in 4-bit on a laptop GPU (§8.5), so its trained numbers are for 4-bit weights. Bits per character is the only quality measure. These results show the direction and rough size of the effect, not the downstream task quality of a properly tuned 7B model.

Data. The social domain is small (189k evaluation characters) and comes from brand-monitoring posts, so brand and product names are over-represented (เบียร์ช้าง, โตโยต้า appear among the added tokens); OPUS-100 is partly subtitle text and its alignments are noisy; the English protect corpus is Wikipedia, so English registers it lacks (chat, legal, informal web) could use entries it never saw. All Thai results are per domain because a pooled average would hide a vocabulary that works for one register only.

Method. Slots are allocated greedily by savings, one merge at a time; an integer program over candidate sets (as BrahmicTokenizer frames it) could do slightly better. Merges are learned from at most 4M characters per domain.

Ethics. All data and models are public; no client or employer data was used. WiseSight posts are user-written: the labels are CC0 but copyright stays with the posters, so they are used only for measurement and never redistributed. The fairness question is treated as a result, not a footnote: §8.2 reports whether the retrofit widens the formal/informal gap, and a formal-only ablation shows what happens when informal Thai is left out of the vocabulary's training data.

10. Conclusion and next steps

Answers to the research questions.

  1. Pre-tokenization vs vocabulary. It depends entirely on the tokenizer. cl100k and Qwen2.5 cut every Thai consonant away from its mark in the pre-tokenizer (\p{L}+ excludes \p{M}); for Qwen2.5 that accounts for 89% of the excess, for cl100k 38%, and cl100k needs both fixes to get anywhere. Mistral-7B, BLOOM and o200k carry all or nearly all of their damage in the vocabulary.
  2. How much a fixed-size retrofit removes. On Mistral-7B, swapping 2,483 entries (7.8%) that English, code and Thai never use removes 64.4% of Thai tokens on Wikipedia, 66.6% on news and 61.1% on social text, takes the Thai penalty from 3.92x to 1.33x and nearly triples effective context, while English moves by +0.01% and 99.55% of English samples get identical token ids. This is far above the plan's 15% target. It should not be set against the Indic 26.7% that motivated the project: that figure compares two different tokenizers, not a tokenizer before and after its retrofit, and a character-level Thai baseline leaves much more to gain than o200k does for Indic scripts. The closest Thai analogue, Csaki et al.'s 73% better fertility on GPT-2, is measured per word.
  3. Against expansion. At Typhoon's own budget the fixed-size retrofit is cheaper than Typhoon on all three domains and ties it on matched content, without growing the embedding matrix. Under strict English-and-code protection Mistral can only free 2,483 slots, and with those it comes within a point of Typhoon.
  4. Who pays. Informal Thai gains less than formal Thai (an informal premium of +11%, the same as Typhoon's +12%), and the premium rises to +21% if the vocabulary is learned from formal text only. The freed slots are paid for by Korean (+39%), Russian (+18%), Chinese (+13%) and Japanese (+10%) text.
  5. What the rows need. Re-setting, and a little tuning. Stale rows nearly double Thai bits per character on BLOOM-560m and random rows quadruple it; the mean of the old pieces recovers most of the loss with no training, and 200 steps of row-only tuning end at least as good as the original on Thai with a third of the tokens and English unchanged. On models that already model Thai well the same recipe falls short: Mistral-7B recovers 45-55% of the damage after 160 steps and Qwen2.5's dead rows 82-87% after 200, both with English unchanged at learning rate 1e-4. Row-only repair is enough for a weak-Thai model; for a strong one the rows need a real training budget - and 1.6M tokens of QLoRA on a laptop GPU brought Mistral-7B within 1-10% of an equally trained control at a third of the Thai tokens (§8.5).

What to do next.

  • Run the embedding repair at the target scale: mean-initialise Mistral-7B's swapped rows and tune only those rows on a GPU for a few thousand steps, then measure downstream Thai tasks, not only bits per character.
  • For Qwen2.5, test the cheapest fix of all - the regex change plus repair of the 1,578 untrained rows - on a larger checkpoint.
  • Build the prune list from a deployment's real traffic (including any Korean, Chinese, Japanese or Russian it serves), and replace greedy allocation with an integer program.
  • Make the cluster-aware variant the default if downstream tools need token boundaries that respect Thai clusters; it costs about one point of reduction.
